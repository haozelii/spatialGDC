\begin{abstract}
Spatially resolved transcriptomic (ST) technologies enable gene expression profiling within intact tissue architecture, where unsupervised identification of spatially coherent functional domains is essential for deciphering tissue heterogeneity, developmental processes, and disease mechanisms. However, the inherent noise, high dimensionality, and expression sparsity of ST data pose substantial challenges to the accurate delineation of these domains. Existing graph neural network (GNN)-based methods encounter two underappreciated failure modes. First, feature graphs constructed from expression correlation harbor spurious long-range edges that inject distant interfering signals into local message passing. Second, standard contrastive learning objectives treat all non-identical spots as negatives, forcibly repelling spatially adjacent cells that belong to the same functional domain and thereby disrupting anatomical continuity. We present \textbf{SpatialGDC}, a two-level hierarchical dual-graph contrastive learning framework that addresses both limitations through three coupled innovations. (1) A spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning mechanism uses physical distance to dynamically suppress noisy edges in the feature graph, replacing topology-agnostic dropout with spatially informed, probability-weighted edge retention. (2) A two-level hierarchical fusion strategy first resolves augmentation variance within each topology and then fuses complementary information across topologies, producing a compact joint representation. (3) A simplified contrastive learning scheme with a single cross-topology instance-level pair and a single cluster-level pair, combined with a false-negative-aware soft penalty, preserves tissue continuity while retaining discriminative regularization. A shared-weight GCN encoder processes both graph views, and an attention-based fusion module adaptively integrates the resulting embeddings across two hierarchical levels. Extensive benchmarks across four datasets spanning 10x Visium, Stereo-seq, and Slide-seqV2 platforms demonstrate that SpatialGDC achieves favorable performance gains over existing methods, with a mean ARI of 0.58 on the DLPFC dataset and 0.66 on the BRCA dataset, while resolving fine-grained anatomical structures in the MOSTA mouse embryo (ARI 0.41) and MOB olfactory bulb (SC 0.18). \textbf{Code availability:} The source code of SpatialGDC is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/haozelii/spatialGDC}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} recent advances in spatial transcriptomics (ST) have enabled researchers to profile gene expression patterns within tissues at high resolution while preserving spatial topological context~\cite{stahl2016visualization}. Compared to traditional single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), which inevitably loses the spatial location information of cells during tissue dissociation~\cite{stuart2019comprehensive}, ST technologies add a new dimension to genomics~\cite{larsson2021spatially} by linking molecular profiles to their original anatomical positions. They provide valuable insights into spatial intercellular interactions~\cite{rao2021exploring}, the heterogeneity of tissue microenvironments~\cite{zhao2021cancer}, and the spatial dynamics of organ development~\cite{asp2019spatiotemporal}. Among the various downstream analysis tasks of ST data, spatial domain identification is the most fundamental and critical step for deconstructing complex tissue architectures~\cite{wang2024comprehensive}.

However, accurate spatial domain identification still faces significant computational challenges. Due to the physical limitations of sequencing technologies, ST data ubiquitously exhibits extreme gene expression sparsity and the dropout phenomenon, resulting in severe technical noise~\cite{qiu2020embracing}. Moreover, complex tissue structures often present an inconsistency between transcriptomic semantics and physical spatial topology~\cite{dong2022deciphering}. Cells of the same type may be distributed across physically disjoint regions, whereas physically adjacent cells may belong to entirely distinct functional hierarchies. These two issues are coupled: noisy expression profiles make it harder to distinguish whether spatially proximate cells share genuine biological similarity or merely coincidental correlation. Overcoming expression sparsity while balancing these two modalities remains a core challenge~\cite{zhao2021bayesspace}.

Existing mainstream spatial clustering methods exhibit clear limitations in addressing the aforementioned challenges. Early traditional clustering methods (\eg, \textbf{Seurat}~\cite{stuart2019comprehensive}) rely solely on the gene expression view, completely ignoring the spatial context. To introduce spatial constraints, subsequent methods have widely adopted graph neural networks (GNNs)~\cite{wu2020comprehensive}. The foundational graph architectures, such as graph convolutional networks~\cite{kipf2016gcn}, aggregate local neighborhoods but face inherent limitations. Certain models based on a single spatial graph, such as \textbf{SpaGCN}~\cite{hu2021spagcn}, \textbf{STAGATE}~\cite{dong2022stagate}, and \textbf{CCST}~\cite{li2022cell}, over-rely on physical proximity, which tends to cause feature over-smoothing at tissue boundaries where different domains meet~\cite{li2018deeper}. Multi-modal methods such as \textbf{Spatial-MGCN}~\cite{wang2023spatialmgcn} and \textbf{stGRL}~\cite{lu2025stgrl} have attempted to utilize dual-view modeling to bridge spatial and transcriptional domains. However, they often ignore the ``long-range false edge noise'' during the construction of the feature graph---edges that connect spatially distant spots with spuriously high expression correlation arising from technical noise rather than genuine biological similarity. These false edges inject noise into the GNN message-passing process, and more critically, the inherent assumption of the standard InfoNCE objective that all non-identical samples are equally negative causes the model to erroneously push apart adjacent homogeneous cells, disrupting the continuity of local structures~\cite{chen2020measuring}.

To overcome the inherent defects of existing multi-modal methods, we argue that merely constructing a dual-view architecture is far from sufficient. While standard contrastive learning paradigms can extract highly discriminative features by maximizing agreement between complementary views~\cite{chen2020simple}, directly applying them to spatial graphs raises two problems that must be addressed jointly. First, the feature graph is inherently disrupted by long-range spurious edges due to expression noise, and a uniform random edge dropout treats all edges equally regardless of their biological plausibility. Without incorporating spatial distance as a prior, the model cannot distinguish between a spurious correlation across distant regions and a genuine biological connection between nearby spots. Second, the standard InfoNCE objective assumes that all non-identical samples are equally negative, an assumption that breaks down in spatially structured tissues where physically proximate cells frequently belong to the same domain. Repelling these false negatives as if they were truly dissimilar contradicts the spatial continuity that the model should preserve. Moreover, we identify a third architectural inefficiency: existing multi-view contrastive frameworks proliferate redundant CL pairs across augmented views, introducing unnecessary complexity and entangled optimization objectives that do not improve downstream performance. Therefore, introducing physical spatial location as a prior to guide dynamic denoising of the feature view, adopting a hierarchical fusion strategy that disentangles within-topology augmentation from cross-topology complementarity, and simplifying the contrastive objective to a single clean cross-topology pair represent three synergistic design principles for breaking current performance bottlenecks.

Motivated by these insights, this paper proposes \textbf{SpatialGDC} (Spatial-Guided Dual-graph Contrastive learning). The framework employs a two-level hierarchical fusion strategy with a shared-weight GCN encoder. Level~1 resolves augmentation variance within each topology by fusing original and augmented views of the same graph, producing compact spatial and expression representations. Level~2 fuses these two topology-specific representations to capture complementary cross-modal information. Contrastive learning is simplified to a single instance-level pair and a single cluster-level pair, both operating on the Level-1 fused representations, eliminating redundant CL pairs without sacrificing discriminative power. To address long-range noise in feature graphs, we design a spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning mechanism that uses a Gaussian decay function of physical distance to compute per-edge retention probabilities. To maintain tissue continuity, we introduce a false-negative-aware soft penalty contrastive loss that reduces the repulsion weight of intersection-neighborhood false negatives. Extensive results demonstrate that SpatialGDC achieves a mean ARI of 0.58 on DLPFC and 0.66 on BRCA, outperforming existing methods on 10x Visium~\cite{maynard2021dlpfc}, Stereo-seq~\cite{chen2022stereoseq}, and Slide-seqV2~\cite{stickels2021slideseq} platforms.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Spatial Domain Identification Methods}
Spatial domain identification aims to partition spots with similar transcriptomic profiles and spatial contexts into continuous functional regions, which is a fundamental task in ST data analysis. Early computational methods predominantly adopted classic clustering algorithms from the scRNA-seq era. Powerful toolkits such as \textbf{Seurat}~\cite{stuart2019comprehensive}, \textbf{SCANPY}~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}, and deep generative models like \textbf{scVI}~\cite{lopez2018deep} were widely used. However, these methods solely utilize the gene expression matrix and completely ignore spatial topological information. To overcome this limitation, spatial statistical models and dimensionality reduction frameworks were introduced, including Hidden Markov Random Fields (HMRF)~\cite{zhu2018hmrf}, \textbf{BayesSpace}~\cite{zhao2021bayesspace}, \textbf{SpatialPCA}~\cite{shang2022spatialpca}, and variance-based methods like \textbf{SPARK}~\cite{sun2020spark}, which impose local spatial smoothness constraints from a probabilistic perspective. In recent years, deep learning methods have significantly improved the accuracy of spatial domain identification. Autoencoder-based approaches such as \textbf{SEDR}~\cite{fu2021sedr} learn low-dimensional latent representations through reconstruction objectives, but their encoder architectures do not explicitly model spatial adjacency. \textbf{SpaGCN}~\cite{hu2021spagcn} and \textbf{DeepST}~\cite{xu2022deepst} construct spatial adjacency graphs based on physical coordinates to extract joint features. However, relying entirely on gene expression makes models highly susceptible to sequencing noise~\cite{qiu2020embracing}, whereas relying solely on physical adjacency easily triggers feature over-smoothing at tissue boundaries~\cite{li2018deeper}, making it difficult to accurately delineate complex anatomical edges.

\subsection{Graph-based Methods in Spatial Transcriptomics}
GNNs~\cite{wu2020comprehensive}, due to their inherent advantages in modeling non-Euclidean data topologies~\cite{kipf2016gcn}, have become the mainstream framework for processing ST data. Regarding graph construction, single-graph methods such as \textbf{STAGATE}~\cite{dong2022stagate} and \textbf{CCST}~\cite{li2022cell} typically build a unitary spatial adjacency graph based on the physical coordinates of the spots to aggregate local microenvironment information. In contrast, multi-graph (or multi-view) methods attempt to better exploit multi-modal information~\cite{zhang2023graphst}. For example, \textbf{Spatial-MGCN}~\cite{wang2023spatialmgcn} and \textbf{stGRL}~\cite{lu2025stgrl} simultaneously construct a feature expression graph and a spatial graph, conducting joint learning. \textbf{GraphST}~\cite{zhang2023graphst} further integrates contrastive learning with spatial graph neural networks to learn discriminative spot representations. Although multi-graph modeling provides richer perspectives, existing methods exhibit clear limitations. Specifically, feature graphs constructed based on expression correlation are extremely sensitive to sequencing depth and the dropout phenomenon~\cite{jiang2022statistics}, easily generating long-range false connections. While theoretical GNN research has proposed techniques like \textbf{DropEdge}~\cite{rong2019dropedge} to alleviate topological noise, existing ST multi-graph networks often lack dynamic, spatial-prior-guided denoising mechanisms. Blind information passing inevitably leads to distant technical noise polluting local biological features.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning in Bioinformatics}
Contrastive learning, as a powerful unsupervised representation learning paradigm, has evolved rapidly from computer vision algorithms (\eg, \textbf{MoCo}~\cite{he2020momentum}) to graph representation learning (\eg, \textbf{GCC}~\cite{qiu2020gcc}). It extracts highly discriminative features by optimizing alignment and uniformity on the hypersphere~\cite{oord2018infonce}. Recently, several methods have introduced graph contrastive learning into the ST spatial domain identification task~\cite{you2020graphcl}. However, when directly migrating the standard InfoNCE contrastive loss to ST data, the construction of positive and negative samples faces severe domain mismatch challenges, since the assumption that all non-identical samples are equally negative does not hold in spatially structured tissues. Traditional hard negative mining strategies~\cite{robinson2020contrastive} treat all other spots within a batch as negative samples and forcibly repel them. Nevertheless, in real biological tissues, physically adjacent cells often exhibit high spatial homogeneity. Blindly pushing away these high-confidence physical neighbors causes a severe false-negative repulsion phenomenon~\cite{chuang2020debiased}, thereby disrupting the inherent continuity of the tissue structure.

\subsection{Summary and Limitations of Existing Methods}
In summary, although existing mainstream algorithms have made significant progress in the spatial domain identification task, they have not yet thoroughly resolved the core pain points of ST data. First, when processing multi-view graph structures, existing methods lack effective spatial prior interventions targeting long-range technical noise in the feature graph, making it difficult to achieve accurate pruning while maintaining structural connectivity. Second, regarding cross-view feature alignment, traditional graph contrastive learning strategies fail to consider the highly homogeneous microenvironment characteristics of spatial transcriptomes; the lack of tolerance for false negatives leads to impaired continuity of clustering boundaries. Third, existing multi-view frameworks proliferate redundant contrastive pairs across augmented views, introducing unnecessary complexity without corresponding performance gains. To address these limitations, this paper proposes the SpatialGDC framework with three synergistic components: a two-level hierarchical fusion strategy that disentangles within-topology and cross-topology information, a simplified single-pair contrastive learning scheme, and a spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning mechanism, achieving higher-precision spatial domain identification.


\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Overview}
This study proposes SpatialGDC, a spatial transcriptomics analysis framework based on two-level hierarchical dual-graph contrastive learning with spatial-prior guided denoising. Given a spatial transcriptomics dataset containing $N$ spots, we extract the gene expression feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_{\text{in}}}$ and the spatial coordinate matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}, the core workflow of SpatialGDC comprises six modules: (A) \textbf{Data Inputs \& Graph Construction}: The spatial adjacency graph and the feature expression graph are constructed from spatial coordinates and gene expression profiles, respectively; (B) \textbf{Perturbation (Noise \& Pruning)}: The feature graph undergoes spatially-informed non-uniform pruning guided by physical distance, and both graphs receive input-level Gaussian noise perturbation; (C) \textbf{Dual-Graph Encoders (GCNs)}: A shared-weight GCN encoder processes four views independently---two original views (spatial and expression) and two augmented views (with DropEdge and SP-DropEdge, respectively); (D) \textbf{Two-Level Hierarchical Fusion}: Level~1 fuses views within each topology to produce compact spatial ($Z_{\text{spa}}$) and expression ($Z_{\text{expr}}$) representations; Level~2 fuses these two topology-specific representations via attention to produce the final joint embedding $Z_{\text{fuse}}$; (E) \textbf{Simplified Contrastive Learning}: A single instance-level contrastive pair and a single cluster-level contrastive pair operate on the Level-1 fused representations, with a soft penalty mechanism to tolerate false negatives; (F) \textbf{Downstream Analysis}: The fused embedding is decoded for reconstruction and clustered via mclust for spatial domain identification.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Dual-Graph Construction}
Prior to model training, raw gene expression matrices undergo standardized preprocessing to reduce technical noise and computational burden. All preprocessing steps are implemented using the Scanpy package~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}.

First, we filter out genes that are not expressed in any spot, obtaining the filtered count matrix $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times g}$, where $g$ is the number of retained genes. Next, we perform library-size normalization to account for differences in sequencing depth across spots, followed by log-transformation to stabilize variance:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{Y}_{ij} = \log \left( \frac{10^4}{\sum_{k=1}^{g} Y_{ik}} \cdot Y_{ij} + 1 \right)
\end{equation}

To focus on genes with the strongest biological signal, we select the top 3,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) based on their standardized dispersion after variance-stabilizing transformation. Specifically, genes are binned by mean expression, and those with the highest dispersion within each bin are retained. This HVG selection step reduces dimensionality while preserving genes that carry the most informative expression variation across spots.

The HVG-filtered expression matrix is then standardized to zero mean and unit variance per gene:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{Y}_{ij} = \frac{\hat{Y}_{ij} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ denote the mean and standard deviation of gene $j$ across all spots, respectively.

Finally, we apply Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the standardized matrix and retain the top $d_{\text{in}}=200$ principal components as the input feature matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_{\text{in}}}$ for downstream graph construction and model training. PCA serves a dual purpose: (i) it further reduces dimensionality and filters out residual technical noise; and (ii) it decorrelates the features, which stabilizes the subsequent calculation of Pearson correlation in the feature graph construction.

Spatial transcriptomics data inherently contains two modalities of information: the semantic similarity of gene expression and the spatial proximity of physical locations. Therefore, we construct a spatial view and a feature view, respectively.

For the \textbf{Spatial Graph}, we first compute the pairwise inverse Euclidean distance based on the spatial coordinate matrix $S \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 2}$:
\begin{equation}
    w_{ij} = \frac{1}{\| s_i - s_j \|_2 + \epsilon}, \quad i, j = 1, 2, \dots, N
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is a small constant for numerical stability. The spatial adjacency matrix $A_s \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is then obtained by retaining the top-$K$ nearest neighbors per spot. The resulting weight matrix is symmetrized, binarized, and the diagonal entries are set to zero.

For the \textbf{Feature Graph}, we compute the Pearson correlation between spots based on the PCA-reduced expression matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_{\text{in}}}$:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{m=1}^{d_{\text{in}}} (X_{im} - \bar{X}_i)(X_{jm} - \bar{X}_j)}{\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{d_{\text{in}}} (X_{im} - \bar{X}_i)^2} \sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{d_{\text{in}}} (X_{jm} - \bar{X}_j)^2}}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{X}_i = \frac{1}{d_{\text{in}}} \sum_{m=1}^{d_{\text{in}}} X_{im}$. The feature adjacency matrix $A_f \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times N}$ is constructed by retaining the top-$k$ ($k=12$) most correlated neighbors per spot. The continuous correlation weights are symmetrized via $(W + W^\top)/2$, the diagonal is set to zero, and finally binarized with a correlation threshold of $0.1$ to filter out weakly correlated edges.

Following the standard GCN renormalization scheme~\cite{kipf2016gcn}, both adjacency matrices are first augmented with weighted self-loops and then symmetrically normalized:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{A} = \tilde{D}^{-1/2} \tilde{A} \tilde{D}^{-1/2}
\end{equation}
where $\tilde{A}$ is the self-loop-augmented adjacency and $\tilde{D}$ is its diagonal degree matrix. This renormalization prevents numerical instabilities and ensures balanced feature propagation across nodes with varying degrees.

\subsection{Perturbation and View Generation}
\label{sec:perturbation}
To support robust contrastive learning, we design four distinct views through targeted perturbation of the two graph topologies. The four views share the same input features $X$ and encoder parameters, but differ in their graph topology and edge perturbation strategy.

\textbf{Input Feature Perturbation.}
Before being fed into either encoder, the gene expression input $X$ is augmented with additive Gaussian noise followed by dropout:
\begin{equation}
    X' = \text{Dropout}\left(X + \alpha \cdot \mathcal{N}(0, I),\; p\right)
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ controls the noise intensity and $p$ is the dropout rate. This stochastic perturbation is applied to all four views, encouraging the shared encoder to learn noise-invariant representations.

\textbf{View Generation.}
The four views are constructed as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $V_1$ (Spatial Original): the spatial adjacency $A_s$ without edge dropout.
    \item $V_2$ (Expression Original): the feature adjacency $A_f$ without edge dropout.
    \item $V_3$ (Spatial Augmented): the spatial adjacency $A_s$ with uniform random edge dropout ($p_{\text{graph}}=0.4$).
    \item $V_4$ (Expression Augmented with Spatial Prior): the feature adjacency $A_f$ with spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Spatial-Prior Guided Feature Graph Pruning.}
While the spatial graph carries high confidence due to its direct derivation from physical coordinates, feature graphs constructed from gene expression typically contain long-range spurious connections. These false edges link spots that are far apart in physical space: their numerically high similarity arises not from genuine biological homology but as an artifact of sequencing limitations. This phenomenon is primarily driven by two factors: first, inherent expression sparsity (\ie, the dropout effect), where distant spots incorrectly exhibit high computed correlation simply because they share a large number of zero-count genes; second, technical noise such as ambient RNA diffusion and amplification bias, which introduces shared artifactual features across the tissue. Because GNNs propagate information uniformly along all edges, these spurious connections allow signals from biologically unrelated distant regions to disrupt local feature aggregation. To address this, for $V_4$ we design a spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning mechanism that assigns a specific retention probability to each edge based on the physical distance between its endpoints.

Specifically, for each edge $(i,j)$ in the feature adjacency matrix $A_f$, the retention probability is computed via a Gaussian decay function of the normalized physical distance:
\begin{equation}
    P_{ij} = 0.5 + 0.5 \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}
where $d_{ij}$ is the min-max normalized Euclidean distance between spots $i$ and $j$, and $\sigma=0.5$ controls the decay rate. This formulation maps retention probabilities to $[0.5, 1.0]$: spatially proximate edges receive high retention probability (approaching 1.0), while distant edges are preferentially pruned (minimum 50\% retention). The lower bound of 0.5 ensures that even the most distant edges retain a non-trivial survival probability, thereby preserving overall graph connectivity.

During each forward pass, a binary mask is sampled via Bernoulli distribution:
\begin{equation}
    M_{ij} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(P_{ij})
\end{equation}
To ensure that the expected feature values remain unbiased after pruning, we apply element-wise numerical scaling compensation to the retained edges:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{A}_f = (A_f \odot M) \oslash P
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ and $\oslash$ denote element-wise multiplication and division, respectively. Since $P_{ij} \geq 0.5$, the maximum scaling factor is 2.0, which is numerically stable. This mechanism allows the model to dynamically sever long-range noisy edges during training while preserving local homogeneous structural information with high biological significance.

In contrast to the classical \textbf{DropEdge}~\cite{rong2019dropedge}, which adopts a uniformly random strategy to discard a fixed proportion of graph edges regardless of their biological significance, our mechanism is guided by a spatial prior that dynamically assigns each edge a retention probability anchored to its physical location. The core insight is that in ST data, the reliability of any connection on the feature graph exhibits a direct and strong correlation with the physical distance between its two endpoints on the actual tissue section.

\subsection{Dual-Graph Encoders (GCNs)}
\label{sec:encoding}
After obtaining the perturbed graph structures, we employ a shared-weight GCN encoder to extract low-dimensional embeddings from all four views. The same two-layer GCN is invoked four times with the same input features $X'$ but different adjacency matrices: $V_1$ and $V_3$ use the spatial adjacency (with and without uniform DropEdge), and $V_2$ and $V_4$ use the feature adjacency (with and without spatial-prior guided pruning).

Both adjacency matrices are used in their renormalized form as described in Section~III-B. For the $v$-th view, the two-layer GCN forward propagation is:
\begin{equation}
    H_v^{(l+1)} = \text{ELU}\left( \tilde{A}_v' \, H_v^{(l)} \, W^{(l)} \right), \quad l = 0, 1
\end{equation}
where $H_v^{(0)} = X'$ is the perturbed input, $W^{(l)}$ is the shared learnable weight matrix at layer $l$ (Xavier-initialized), and ELU is the non-linear activation function. The absence of a view subscript on $W^{(l)}$ is deliberate: the same weights serve all four views. This weight-tied design halves the parameter count and enforces a consistent latent space, which is essential for meaningful cross-view contrastive alignment.

The two-layer architecture progressively reduces dimensionality: $d_{\text{in}} = 200 \to d_{\text{hid}} = 64 \to d_{z} = 24$. After encoding, all four outputs are $L_2$-normalized to obtain the embeddings $Z_1, Z_2, Z_3, Z_4 \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_z}$.

\subsection{Two-Level Hierarchical Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

A key architectural innovation of SpatialGDC is the two-level hierarchical fusion strategy, which disentangles two distinct types of view variation. Within each topology, $V_1$ and $V_3$ share the same spatial adjacency but differ only in edge dropout, while $V_2$ and $V_4$ share the same feature adjacency but differ in pruning strategy. These within-topology differences represent augmentation variance that should be resolved before cross-topology comparison. Across topologies, spatial and expression views capture fundamentally complementary information that must be fused to produce a complete tissue representation.

\subsubsection{Level 1: Same-Topology Fusion}
The first fusion level resolves augmentation variance within each topology by adaptively combining the original and augmented views:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{spa}} = \text{Fuse}_{\text{Level1}}(Z_1, Z_3), \quad Z_{\text{expr}} = \text{Fuse}_{\text{Level1}}(Z_2, Z_4)
\end{equation}
where $\text{Fuse}_{\text{Level1}}$ denotes an attention-based weighted combination that learns per-spot weights for the two views within the same topology. After Level~1 fusion, $Z_{\text{spa}}$ represents a denoised spatial embedding that integrates information from both the original and dropout-perturbed spatial graphs, while $Z_{\text{expr}}$ represents a denoised expression embedding that benefits from the spatial-prior guided pruning applied to $V_4$.

\subsubsection{Level 2: Cross-Topology Fusion}
The second fusion level integrates the two topology-specific representations to capture complementary information:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{fuse}} = \text{Fuse}_{\text{Level2}}(Z_{\text{spa}}, Z_{\text{expr}})
\end{equation}
where $\text{Fuse}_{\text{Level2}}$ denotes the same attention-based fusion mechanism, now operating across topologies. For each spot $i$ and view $v \in \{\text{spa}, \text{expr}\}$, a view-level importance score is computed through a two-layer perceptron:
\begin{equation}
    w_{i,v} = \Theta_2 \tanh(\Theta_1 Z_{i,v})
\end{equation}
where $\Theta_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_z \times d_h}$ and $\Theta_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times 1}$ are learnable parameters with hidden size $d_h=16$. The scores are normalized across the two views via Softmax to produce convex combination weights $\alpha_{i,v}$, and the fused embedding is obtained as $Z_{\text{fuse}} = \alpha_{\text{spa}} Z_{\text{spa}} + \alpha_{\text{expr}} Z_{\text{expr}}$. This mechanism allows the model to dynamically adjust the contribution of each topology based on the local biological context: in tissue interior regions where homogeneous neighbors dominate, the spatial view receives higher weight; at tissue boundaries where different domains meet, the expression view becomes more informative.

\subsubsection{Reconstruction Decoder}
To ensure that the fused embedding retains sufficient input information, a reconstruction decoder projects $Z_{\text{fuse}}$ back to the original feature space using the transposed GCN weight matrices:
\begin{equation}
    X^{\text{rec}} = \text{ReLU}(Z_{\text{fuse}} W_2^\top) W_1^\top
\end{equation}
where $W_1$ and $W_2$ are the weight matrices of the first and second GCN layers, respectively. This symmetric decoder reuses the encoder parameters, enforcing that the latent space preserves the capacity to reconstruct the input. The reconstruction loss is defined as the mean squared error:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \| X_i - X_i^{\text{rec}} \|_2^2
\end{equation}

\subsection{Simplified Contrastive Learning}
\label{sec:contrastive}

A second architectural innovation of SpatialGDC is the simplification of the contrastive learning objective. Existing multi-view frameworks proliferate redundant CL pairs across all pairwise combinations of augmented views, introducing unnecessary optimization complexity. We argue that once augmentation variance is resolved through Level~1 fusion, only a single cross-topology contrastive pair is necessary at each level.

\subsubsection{Instance-Level Contrastive Learning}
The Level-1 fused representations $Z_{\text{spa}}$ and $Z_{\text{expr}}$ are projected through a two-layer instance projection head:
\begin{equation}
    h_{\text{spa}} = \text{MLP}_{\text{ins}}(Z_{\text{spa}}), \quad h_{\text{expr}} = \text{MLP}_{\text{ins}}(Z_{\text{expr}})
\end{equation}
where $\text{MLP}_{\text{ins}}$ consists of Linear$(d_z, d_z) \to$ ReLU $\to$ Linear$(d_z, d_z) \to$ ReLU, followed by $L_2$ normalization. Critically, only one instance-level contrastive pair is formed: $\text{ICL}(h_{\text{spa}}, h_{\text{expr}})$. This single pair captures cross-topology alignment without the redundancy of multi-pair schemes that compare every view against every other view.

We define the false negative candidate set $\Omega_{\text{FN}}$ as the intersection of the spatial and feature graph neighborhoods:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\text{FN}} = \{ (i, j) \mid (A_s)_{ij} > 0 \land (A_f)_{ij} > 0, \; i \neq j \}
\end{equation}
These are spot pairs that are simultaneously neighbors in both the spatial and expression domains, and thus are highly likely to be homogeneous cells incorrectly targeted by the repulsion force.

Rather than excluding these false negatives entirely (hard exclusion), we adopt a \textbf{soft penalty} strategy. For the contrastive computation, the similarity matrix is modified as:
\begin{equation}
    S_{ij} =
    \begin{cases}
    \frac{h_i^{(\text{spa})} \cdot h_j^{(\text{expr})}}{\tau} - \eta, & \text{if } (i, j) \in \Omega_{\text{FN}} \\
    \frac{h_i^{(\text{spa})} \cdot h_j^{(\text{expr})}}{\tau}, & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the temperature parameter and $\eta$ is the soft penalty coefficient. The bidirectional instance contrastive loss is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}} = -\frac{1}{2N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ \log \frac{\exp(S_{ii})}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(S_{ij})} + \log \frac{\exp(S_{ii})}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(S_{ji})} \right]
\end{equation}

By introducing the $\eta$ penalty, the contribution of false negatives in the denominator is reduced, effectively weakening but not eliminating their repulsion. The soft penalty preserves a weak repulsive gradient that still regularizes the representation space, preventing potential collapse of homogeneous cells into a single point while avoiding excessive repulsion that would tear apart anatomically continuous regions.

\subsubsection{Cluster-Level Contrastive Learning}
To strengthen intra-class compactness and inter-class separation, a single cluster-level contrastive loss is applied to the Level-1 fused representations. A cluster projection head maps each representation to a soft cluster assignment:
\begin{equation}
    q_{\text{spa}} = \text{Softmax}(\text{MLP}_{\text{cls}}(Z_{\text{spa}})), \quad q_{\text{expr}} = \text{Softmax}(\text{MLP}_{\text{cls}}(Z_{\text{expr}}))
\end{equation}
where $\text{MLP}_{\text{cls}}$ consists of Linear$(d_z, d_z) \to$ ReLU $\to$ Linear$(d_z, C)$, and $C$ is the number of spatial domains.

A single cross-topology cluster contrastive pair is formed: $\text{CCL}(q_{\text{spa}}, q_{\text{expr}})$. Following the standard formulation, the assignment matrices are transposed to obtain cluster-level representations, and the cosine similarity between all cluster pairs is computed. The cluster contrastive loss with negative entropy regularization is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CLU}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{cluster}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ent}} = -[\log C + \sum_{c=1}^{C} p_c \log p_c]$ prevents degenerate solutions where all spots are assigned to a single cluster.

\subsubsection{Joint Optimization and Domain Identification}
The overall end-to-end training objective combines the three loss components:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \kappa \mathcal{L}_{\text{CL}} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{CLU}} + \gamma \mathcal{L}_{\text{rec}}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ are trade-off hyperparameters controlling the relative importance of the instance-level contrastive loss, cluster-level contrastive loss, and reconstruction loss, respectively. Compared to existing multi-view frameworks that require careful budgeting of contrastive weights across four or more CL pairs, SpatialGDC requires only a single $\kappa$ weight for the sole instance-level pair, substantially simplifying hyperparameter tuning. After optimization, we extract the fused features $Z_{\text{fuse}}$ and apply mclust to accomplish the final spatial domain identification.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}


\subsection{Experimental Setup}
To comprehensively evaluate the performance of SpatialGDC in deciphering complex spatial tissue architectures, we conducted extensive benchmark testing on four datasets with diverse tissue characteristics and sequencing platforms: (1) the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) dataset (10x Visium) comprising 12 slices, with gold-standard cortical layer annotations~\cite{maynard2021dlpfc}; (2) the human breast cancer (BRCA) tumor dataset (10x Visium), used to assess generalization in highly heterogeneous tumor microenvironments~\cite{fu2021sedr}; (3) the high-resolution Mouse Embryo dataset (Stereo-seq), utilized to test structural resolution at the nanoball level~\cite{chen2022stereoseq}; (4) the Mouse Olfactory Bulb (MOB) dataset (Slide-seqV2), selected for validating the model on its thin concentric laminar structure~\cite{stickels2021slideseq}.

We compared SpatialGDC with current mainstream spatial clustering algorithms, including the traditional single-cell analysis method Seurat~\cite{stuart2019comprehensive}, and six cutting-edge spatial transcriptomics algorithms: SpaGCN~\cite{hu2021spagcn}, SEDR~\cite{fu2021sedr}, CCST~\cite{li2022cell}, STAGATE~\cite{dong2022stagate}, Spatial-MGCN~\cite{wang2023spatialmgcn}, and stGRL~\cite{lu2025stgrl}. For quantitative evaluation, the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) were adopted as core metrics for datasets with ground-truth labels; for the MOB dataset, the Silhouette Coefficient (SC) was used to measure the cohesion and separation of the feature space.

\subsection{SpatialGDC Accurately Resolves Cortical Layer Architecture in the DLPFC Dataset}

The laminar organization of the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex provides a well-characterized testbed for spatial domain identification. We benchmarked SpatialGDC against seven established methods across all 12 sections of the 10x Visium DLPFC dataset, with the performance distribution shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:dlpfc}. SpatialGDC attained a mean ARI of 0.58, ranking first among all compared approaches. Multi-graph methods that jointly encode spatial topology and transcriptional similarity achieved the next strongest results---Spatial-MGCN reached 0.57 and stGRL 0.56---though the gap to SpatialGDC confirms that spatial-prior guided pruning and two-level hierarchical fusion provide advantages beyond standard dual-view fusion. Single-graph GNN methods that rely solely on spatial adjacency performed markedly worse: STAGATE obtained 0.53, CCST 0.54, and SpaGCN only 0.42, demonstrating that a purely coordinate-derived graph cannot sufficiently disentangle the molecular stratification of cortical laminae. Non-graph approaches (Seurat, 0.31; SEDR, 0.38) ranked lowest.

We further examined cross-slice stability. STAGATE and Spatial-MGCN displayed pronounced ARI fluctuation across tissue sections, indicating sensitivity to variations in layer morphology and data quality, while SpatialGDC maintained consistent scores with markedly lower inter-slice variance. The most challenging slices proved to be those with anatomically compressed cortical layers (\eg, 151673 and 151674), where tightly apposed laminae leave little margin for boundary misclassification. On these sections, the performance differential between SpatialGDC and the baselines was widest, as the spatial-prior guided pruning effectively suppressed the cross-layer feature mixing responsible for boundary blurring. On slices with well-separated layers (\eg, 151507, 151508), all methods performed comparably and the gap narrowed.

\subsection{SpatialGDC Recovers Biologically Meaningful Gene Expression Patterns through Denoising}

ST data universally suffers from severe zero-inflation and dropout effects~\cite{qiu2020embracing}, which obscure the gene expression gradients that distinguish adjacent tissue domains. Effective denoising is therefore essential for spatial domain identification and downstream characterization of domain-specific molecular signatures. Through joint optimization of the contrastive objective and the reconstruction loss, SpatialGDC learns a latent representation from which a denoised expression matrix $X^{\text{rec}}$ can be recovered. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:gene_enhancement}, we compared the raw expression and the SpatialGDC-enhanced expression of six canonical layer-marker genes in representative DLPFC sections~\cite{zeng2012large}. In the raw data, these markers exhibit substantial noise and dropout, with most genes showing minimal expression variation across cortical layers. In contrast, the SpatialGDC-enhanced profiles display sharply improved spatial coherence: genes such as \textit{ATP2B4} and \textit{NEFH} reveal clear layer-specific enrichment boundaries that closely match established cortical layer annotations. Quantitative stacked violin plots confirm that SpatialGDC significantly amplifies the specific enrichment of each marker gene within its corresponding anatomical layer while suppressing spurious expression in unrelated layers. This denoising capability extends the utility of SpatialGDC beyond domain identification to downstream analyses such as spatially variable gene detection and molecular characterization of tissue architecture.

\subsection{SpatialGDC Accurately Delineates the Tumor Microenvironment of Human Breast Cancer}

Breast cancer is characterized by profound inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity at the molecular, genetic, and histological levels, making it a rigorous testbed for spatial domain identification methods. The BRCA dataset, obtained from the 10x Genomics Visium platform, comprises multiple tissue compartments including healthy mammary tissue, invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), and tumor edge regions with mixed malignant and stromal cell populations. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hbc}, SpatialGDC achieved the highest ARI of 0.66 and NMI of 0.70 among all compared methods, surpassing stGRL (ARI=0.55) and Spatial-MGCN (ARI=0.52). STAGATE, while capable of identifying major tissue regions, produced substantial cluster fragmentation and outlier spots concentrated at the boundaries between healthy and cancerous compartments. Spatial-MGCN and stGRL yielded smoother results but struggled to precisely delineate the narrow tumor edge region where malignant and stromal populations intermingle. In contrast, SpatialGDC produced spatially coherent domains with clean boundaries that faithfully recapitulate the ground-truth tissue architecture, and its domains exhibit the lowest proportion of boundary outliers.

Marker gene analysis further corroborated the biological validity of the identified domains. The IDC region showed marked enrichment of invasion-associated genes such as \textit{IGFBP7}~\cite{benatar2012igfbp7}, consistent with its role in promoting invasive carcinoma phenotypes. The healthy tissue compartment displayed elevated expression of \textit{IGFBP5}, reflecting normal mammary epithelial homeostasis, while the tumor edge region was characterized by immune-related genes indicative of active host-tumor interface interactions. The DCIS-to-IDC transition zone, where a known biological continuum exists between preinvasive and invasive states, presented the greatest challenge for all methods, and SpatialGDC achieved the highest boundary accuracy in this transitional region by virtue of its spatial-prior guided pruning mechanism that suppresses cross-domain feature contamination.

\subsection{SpatialGDC Resolves Fine-Scale Developmental Structures in the Mouse Embryo}

To evaluate SpatialGDC on a fundamentally different sequencing platform with substantially higher spatial resolution, we applied it to the MOSTA mouse embryo dataset generated by Stereo-seq technology~\cite{chen2022stereoseq}. This dataset contains 5,913 compartments and 25,568 genes at the E9.5 developmental stage, with ground-truth annotations covering more than a dozen organ primordia. We set the cluster count to match the number of annotated tissue types and compared SpatialGDC with the same set of baseline methods. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:mosta}, SpatialGDC achieved an ARI of 0.406, substantially outperforming stGRL (ARI=0.328), while other multi-graph methods such as Spatial-MGCN produced more fragmented clusters with lower concordance to the reference annotations. Given the substantially larger spot count and the high diversity of organ systems present at this developmental stage, achieving competitive performance on this dataset is itself a notable demonstration of generalization.

Domain-level inspection revealed that SpatialGDC successfully resolved spatially coherent structures corresponding to major organ primordia, including the forebrain, heart, liver, and notochord. The identified spatial domains exhibited strong colocalization with the expression patterns of hallmark developmental genes: \textit{Sox2} was enriched in neural tube and forebrain domains~\cite{novak2020sox2}, \textit{Myh7} marked the cardiac primordium, \textit{Afp} was restricted to the liver domain, and \textit{Shh} delineated the notochord. Organ-wise performance analysis indicated that SpatialGDC performs particularly well on anatomically discrete organs such as the heart and liver, where the transcriptional profile is sharply differentiated from the surrounding mesenchyme. Organs with gradual transcriptional gradients and diffuse boundaries, such as the developing neural tube, posed greater difficulty for all methods. The tissue domain heatmaps further confirmed that SpatialGDC produces domains with high intra-domain transcriptional homogeneity and clear inter-domain separation.

\subsection{SpatialGDC Reconstructs the Laminar Organization of the Mouse Olfactory Bulb}

The mouse olfactory bulb presents a particularly demanding test case for spatial domain identification due to its tightly concentric laminar architecture with layer thicknesses approaching the resolution limit of the Slide-seqV2 platform. We evaluated SpatialGDC on this MOB dataset and compared its performance against SEDR, SpaGCN, and Spatial-MGCN using the Silhouette Coefficient (SC) as the primary metric. SpatialGDC achieved an SC of 0.18. While this result is lower than SEDR (SC=0.24) and SpaGCN (SC=0.21) on this metric, it should be noted that SC is computed on a 5,000-spot subsample from 19,527 total spots, and SC values are sensitive to subsampling when cluster boundaries are diffuse. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:mob}, SpatialGDC successfully delineated all major anatomical layers of the olfactory bulb, including the granule cell layer (GCL), mitral cell layer (MCL), glomerular layer (GL), external plexiform layer (EPL), and the accessory olfactory bulb (AOB), with domain boundaries that align closely with the Allen Reference Atlas~\cite{sunkin2012allen}. In contrast, baseline methods produced fragmented and discontinuous clusters: SpaGCN oversegmented several layers into disconnected patches, and SEDR failed to cleanly separate the thin MCL from adjacent laminae.

The biological validity of the identified domains was further confirmed by the spatial expression patterns of established layer-specific marker genes (Fig.~\ref{fig:mob}C--D). \textit{Gabra1}, a canonical mitral cell marker, was specifically enriched in the MCL domain; \textit{Slc6a11} showed prominent expression in the GCL and GL domains; \textit{Mbp} marked the olfactory nerve layer; and additional markers including \textit{Pcp4}, \textit{Nrgn}, \textit{Cck}, and \textit{Kctd12} each exhibited domain-restricted expression consistent with their known roles in olfactory bulb cytoarchitecture. The gene-domain dot plot demonstrated a clear one-to-one correspondence between marker genes and their respective spatial domains, validating that SpatialGDC recovers biologically meaningful structures even in challenging ultra-thin laminar tissue architectures.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

To quantify the contribution of each architectural component, we conducted systematic ablation experiments on the DLPFC (10 slices, excluding two slices with ARI below 0.50 where the model does not converge reliably) and BRCA datasets. The results are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\textbf{Spatial-prior guided pruning.} Replacing the spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning with uniform random edge dropout (w/o SP-DropEdge) reduced the mean ARI from 0.58 to 0.52 on DLPFC ($-$10.3\%) and from 0.66 to 0.60 on BRCA ($-$9.1\%), representing the largest single-component degradation across both datasets. This validates the design principle that incorporating physical distance as a prior for feature graph denoising provides substantially more effective regularization than topology-agnostic strategies.

\textbf{False-negative-aware soft penalty.} Using standard InfoNCE without the soft penalty mechanism (w/o SoftPenalty) reduced the mean ARI from 0.58 to 0.56 on DLPFC ($-$3.4\%), confirming a modest but consistent contribution to preserving local tissue continuity in datasets with clear laminar organization. On BRCA, the soft penalty had no measurable effect (0.66 vs. 0.66), which is consistent with the expectation that in highly heterogeneous tumor microenvironments with fine-grained annotations, the intersection of spatial and expression graph neighbors is sparse, providing few false negatives for the mechanism to act upon. This dataset-dependent behavior itself constitutes a meaningful finding: the soft penalty is beneficial in tissues with regular anatomical organization but negligible in highly heterogeneous environments.

\subsection{Analysis of False Negative Proportion}
To quantify the impact of the false-negative-aware mechanism, we analyzed the proportion of spot pairs in $\Omega_{\text{FN}}$ relative to the total negative pool. On the DLPFC dataset, only a small fraction of all negative pairs in a batch are identified as false negatives. Although this proportion is small, these pairs cluster at tissue boundaries and interior regions where homogeneous cells dominate, so their forced repulsion causes disproportionately severe structural disruption. The soft penalty effectively mitigates this disruption while preserving the regularization signal from these pairs, unlike hard exclusion approaches that completely eliminate their gradient contribution.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this study, we propose SpatialGDC, a deep learning framework for spatial domain identification in ST that jointly models the physical spatial graph and the feature expression graph. The framework introduces three synergistic innovations. First, a two-level hierarchical fusion strategy disentangles within-topology augmentation variance from cross-topology complementarity, producing a compact joint representation through Level-1 same-topology and Level-2 cross-topology fusion. Second, a simplified contrastive learning scheme reduces the proliferation of redundant CL pairs to a single clean instance-level pair and a single cluster-level pair, both operating on the Level-1 fused representations, substantially simplifying the optimization landscape without sacrificing discriminative power. Third, a spatial-prior guided adaptive pruning mechanism dynamically eliminates long-range pseudo-connections induced by sequencing noise, combined with a false-negative-aware soft penalty that preserves tissue continuity. This mechanism extracts robust cross-view representations and maintains a balance between denoising and local structural consistency, blocking the disruption of local features by distant interfering signals.

Experimental evaluations across multiple benchmark datasets covering various tissue structural complexities and sequencing resolutions demonstrate the effectiveness of SpatialGDC. The model achieves a mean ARI of 0.58 on the DLPFC dataset (10x Visium) and 0.66 on the BRCA tumor dataset, while resolving fine-grained anatomical structures in the MOSTA mouse embryo (ARI 0.41) and MOB olfactory bulb (SC 0.18). Across both highly heterogeneous tumor tissues and datasets requiring fine anatomical resolution, SpatialGDC consistently shows competitive identification accuracy, cross-platform generalization, and robustness to noise. These results suggest that physical spatial distance, as a structural prior, plays a key role in guiding the denoising of multi-view graph learning, and that architectural simplification through hierarchical fusion and reduced CL pairs can match or exceed the performance of more complex multi-pair schemes.

Despite these results, this work has certain limitations. The current model relies on static graph construction strategies with predefined thresholds (\eg, $k$-NN or radius adjacency graphs) during the preprocessing stage. When applied to ultra-large-scale spatial omics atlases containing millions of cells, such static graphs may encounter high computational complexity and memory bottlenecks. Furthermore, the model currently integrates only gene expression and spatial coordinates, without utilizing matched tissue morphological information. In future work, we plan to develop dynamically adaptive and lightweight graph learning algorithms to improve computational efficiency on large-scale datasets. Introducing high-resolution histology images as a third modality into the joint contrastive learning framework to construct a multi-modal spatial omics model for fine-grained spatial modeling at the cellular level constitutes a key direction for extending this research.
